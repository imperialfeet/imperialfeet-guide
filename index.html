Bunions: Causes, Pain Relief and Treatment Options

An in-depth foot-health guide — last updated October 2026

If you have ever felt a dull ache at the base of your big toe after a long day on your feet, or noticed a firm bump gradually nudging that toe out of line, you may be looking at the early stages of a bunion. Bunions are among the most common foot problems in adults, and although they develop slowly, they can quietly reshape your comfort, your gait and even the shoes you are able to wear.

The reassuring news is that bunions are very well understood, and the majority of people manage them successfully without surgery. Knowing what causes them, how to calm the pain at home, and when to ask a professional for help can make a real difference to your everyday quality of life. This in-depth guide from Imperial Feet explains what bunions are, why they form, the practical steps you can take to relieve the discomfort, and how to protect your feet for the long term — so you can stay active and comfortable on your feet.

What Exactly Is a Bunion?

A bunion, known in medical terms as hallux valgus, is a bony prominence that develops at the joint where the big toe meets the foot, called the metatarsophalangeal joint. It forms when some of the bones in the forefoot gradually shift out of their normal position. As this happens, the tip of the big toe is pulled toward the smaller toes, while the joint at the base of the big toe is pushed outward, creating the characteristic bump on the inner edge of the foot.

Over time, that misaligned joint can become inflamed, sore and reddened, particularly where it presses against the inside of a shoe. The skin over the bump often thickens into a callus, and the big toe may begin to overlap or tuck beneath the second toe. In more advanced cases, the changed mechanics of the forefoot can cause pain under the ball of the foot, encourage hammertoe deformities in neighbouring toes, and subtly alter the way you walk.

It is worth noting that a similar, smaller bump can appear at the base of the little toe. Often called a bunionette or tailor’s bunion, it follows the same principles and responds to much the same care. Bunions never appear overnight; they develop gradually over months and years, which is precisely why early attention pays off — the sooner you address the contributing factors, the better your chance of slowing the progression.

What Causes Bunions?

Bunions rarely have a single cause. In most cases they are the result of several influences acting together over many years — the shoes you wear, the feet you inherited, and the way your feet are built and move.

Footwear

Footwear is the factor most people suspect first, and it does play a genuine role. Shoes with narrow, pointed toe boxes squeeze the toes together and force the big toe toward the second toe, while high heels shift a disproportionate share of your body weight onto the forefoot and increase pressure on the big-toe joint. Wearing such shoes day after day, year after year, can accelerate a bunion that might otherwise have remained mild.

That said, shoes alone do not usually create a bunion from nothing. Plenty of people wear fashionable footwear for decades without developing one, which tells us that shoes act more as an aggravating influence on feet that are already susceptible. The practical takeaway is straightforward: roomy, supportive footwear will not guarantee you never develop a bunion, but cramped, unsupportive shoes can certainly make an existing tendency worse, and faster.

Genetics and Family History

If bunions run in your family, your own risk is noticeably higher. Research into family patterns suggests that the tendency to develop bunions is inherited — not the bunion itself, but the foot shape and joint flexibility that allow one to form. Children of parents with bunions often show the same foot type long before any bump appears.

This hereditary link helps explain why bunions can appear even in people who have always worn sensible shoes. If your mother, father or grandparents dealt with bunions, it is wise to pay attention to the early signs — such as the big toe beginning to drift — and to choose footwear with a generous toe box before problems start rather than after.

Foot Structure and Mechanics

The architecture of your feet matters enormously. People with flat feet or very low arches, excessively flexible ligaments, or an abnormal walking pattern place extra stress on the big-toe joint with every step. Over thousands of steps a day, that repeated stress can gradually push the joint out of alignment.

Certain forms of arthritis, particularly inflammatory types such as rheumatoid arthritis, can also damage the joint and encourage bunion formation. Previous foot injuries — a fracture or sprain that healed with slightly altered mechanics — may contribute too. In short, any condition that changes how force travels through the forefoot can, over time, nudge the big toe off its proper line.

Common Symptoms of Bunions

You may not notice a bunion at first, because the earliest stage can be painless. The signs that typically bring it to attention include:

  • A visible bony bump on the inner side of the foot at the base of the big toe
  • Aching, soreness or a burning sensation around the big-toe joint, often worse after long periods of standing or walking
  • Swelling, redness or warmth over the joint
  • Thickened skin, calluses or corns where the big toe rubs against the second toe or the shoe
  • Stiffness or restricted movement in the big toe
  • Pain in the ball of the foot, or hammertoe deformities developing as pressure shifts across the forefoot
  • Increasing difficulty finding shoes that fit comfortably without pressing on the bump

Symptoms tend to come and go at first, flaring after a busy day and settling with rest. As the deformity progresses, however, the pain can become more persistent — a clear signal that it is time to take the condition seriously and seek professional advice.

Conservative Pain Relief: What You Can Do at Home

Surgery is rarely the first answer. For most people, a combination of simple, consistent measures keeps bunion pain well under control and can slow further progression. Here are the strategies with the best track record.

1. Change Your Shoes

Start with your shoes, because this is the single change that brings the fastest relief. Choose footwear with a wide, deep toe box that lets your toes lie flat and spread naturally, without the sides of the shoe pressing on the bunion. Keep heels low — ideally under five centimetres — since high heels drive weight forward onto the sore joint. Look for soft, flexible uppers, such as leather or stretch materials, that give a little over the bump rather than rubbing it raw. Make sure both shoes fit properly; feet swell during the day, so shopping for shoes in the afternoon gives a truer fit. If you must wear dress shoes for an occasion, limit the time you spend in them and change into supportive footwear afterwards.

2. Padding and Supportive Devices

Cushioning the bump reduces the daily friction that causes most bunion pain. Over-the-counter bunion pads — felt, foam or gel — shield the joint from shoe pressure, while soft toe spacers placed between the big and second toes can ease crowding and encourage better alignment. Custom or prefabricated orthotic insoles help too, especially if flat feet or faulty mechanics are part of the picture; by supporting the arch and distributing pressure more evenly, they take load off the vulnerable joint. In some cases a clinician may recommend taping or splinting the foot into a more normal position, particularly overnight.

3. Ice and Rest

When the joint flares up — hot, swollen and throbbing after a long day — cold therapy is your friend. Apply an ice pack wrapped in a thin towel for fifteen to twenty minutes, a few times a day, to calm inflammation. Elevating the foot and simply resting it after aggravation helps as well. Over-the-counter anti-inflammatory medication can ease short-term pain and swelling, but it is sensible to check with a pharmacist or doctor first, especially if you take other medicines or have an ongoing health condition.

4. Exercises and Stretches

Gentle exercises keep the joint mobile and strengthen the small muscles that support it. Try these for a few minutes each day: spread all your toes as wide as possible and hold for five seconds; pick up small objects such as marbles with your toes, or scrunch up a towel laid flat on the floor; roll the sole of your foot over a tennis ball or a frozen water bottle to massage the tissues; and stretch the calf muscles against a wall, since tight calves alter foot mechanics. None of these will straighten an established bunion, but they can relieve stiffness, improve comfort and support healthier foot function.

When Is Surgery Considered?

Most bunions never require an operation. Surgery generally enters the conversation only when conservative measures have been given a fair trial — typically many months — and significant problems persist. The usual triggers include pain that limits everyday activities such as walking or working despite proper footwear and supports; chronic inflammation or swelling that does not settle; a big toe that continues to drift and crowd the other toes; or stiffness in the joint so severe that it interferes with movement.

It is important to understand that bunion surgery is performed to relieve pain and restore function, not for cosmetic reasons alone — reputable surgeons will not operate on a painless bunion simply because of its appearance. Procedures vary depending on severity, but most involve realigning the bones of the forefoot, and they range from minimally invasive techniques to more involved reconstructions. Recovery takes commitment: expect several weeks of limited weight-bearing, followed by a gradual return to normal footwear. Anyone considering surgery should discuss the expected benefits, the recovery timeline and the small but real risks with a qualified foot specialist before making a decision.

How to Prevent Bunions

You cannot rewrite your genetics, but you can substantially lower your odds of developing a painful bunion — or stop a mild one from worsening. The foundations are familiar by now: wear shoes with a roomy toe box and sensible heels as your everyday default; keep your weight in a healthy range so your forefoot carries less load; and stay active with foot-strengthening exercises. If you have flat feet, supportive insoles or orthotics can correct the mechanics that drive bunion formation. Pay attention to your children’s footwear too, since habits formed young shape adult feet. And above all, do not ignore the early warning signs — a toe that is starting to drift, or a joint that aches after shoe pressure, deserves action now rather than resignation later.

Caring for Your Whole Foot

Caring for your feet goes beyond any single condition. Healthy daily habits — washing and thoroughly drying your feet, trimming toenails straight across, moisturising dry skin and rotating your shoes — protect against a whole range of common problems. If you are dealing with thickened, discoloured or brittle toenails alongside your bunion concerns, a targeted nail fungus treatment used consistently can help restore clearer, healthier-looking nails. Small, steady steps in foot care tend to pay off far more than any single quick fix.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can bunions go away on their own?

No. Once the joint has shifted out of alignment, it will not straighten itself, and the deformity tends to progress slowly over time. However, “not reversible” does not mean “not manageable” — the pain-relief strategies in this guide can keep most bunions comfortable for years, and early action can meaningfully slow their progression.

Are bunions hereditary?

There is a strong family link. What you inherit is not the bunion itself but the foot shape, joint flexibility and walking pattern that make one likely. If close relatives have bunions, choosing roomy footwear early and monitoring your feet gives you the best chance of staying ahead of the problem.

What is the best type of shoe for bunions?

Look for a wide, deep toe box, a low heel, a supportive arch and a soft upper that will not rub the joint. Lace-up or adjustable shoes let you fine-tune the fit around the bump. Avoid pointed toes and very high heels for daily wear — save them for brief occasions if you wear them at all.

When should I see a doctor about a bunion?

Book an appointment if pain persists despite several weeks of self-care, if the joint is frequently swollen or inflamed, if the big toe is visibly pushing the other toes out of position, or if the bunion is interfering with walking, working or wearing normal shoes. Earlier assessment generally means simpler treatment.

Does walking barefoot help or hurt bunions?

It depends on the surface and your feet. Walking barefoot on soft, natural surfaces lets the toes spread and can strengthen foot muscles, which many people find helpful. But on hard floors, barefoot walking offers no cushioning or arch support, which can aggravate an already sore joint — supportive footwear or cushioned slippers are usually the kinder option indoors.

The Bottom Line

Bunions are common, but they do not have to dictate how you live. With sensible footwear, simple home measures and timely professional advice when needed, most people keep bunion pain firmly under control and stay active for years.
